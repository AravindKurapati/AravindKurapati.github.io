<p>A third-year undergrad’s honest account of benchmarking six neural networks, publishing two papers, and learning ML/DL before ChatGPT existed.</p><p>Let me rewind to explain how three college kids who were only busy mastering PUBG headshots somehow ended up publishing lung cancer detection papers.</p><h3>From PUBG Headshots to Research Papers (A Completely Expected Transition)</h3><p>March 2020. End of second year. COVID lockdown hits.</p><p>Our daily schedule looked like this:</p><ul><li>10 AM: Wake up</li><li>2:30PM: Among Us with the squad</li><li>6PM: PUBG (the focus, the tension, the dedication)</li></ul><figure><img loading="lazy" alt="" src="/writing/when-stack-overflow-was-our-only-friend-teaching-ourselves/010d33de9107.png" /><figcaption>shout-out to the 🐐🐐</figcaption></figure><ul><li>11PM: More PUBG</li><li>2AM: Get caught playing by parents, pretend to sleep</li><li>3AM: Back to PUBG with Discord muted</li></ul><p>Those were genuinely the days. Late night talks, stupid silly jokes, and the kind of friendship that forms when you’re all stuck at home with nothing but video games and terrible WiFi.</p><p>Research papers? Career planning? Future goals? Absolutely not on our radar.</p><p>Then one day, my friend’s parents came to visit. His mom, <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/sejal-nimbhorkar/">Dr. Sejal Santosh Nimbhorkar</a>, was a professor at a Bangalore university. She asked us the question that every Indian parent eventually asks: “What are you planning to do in the future? Any plans to publish research papers?”</p><p>We stared at her like she’d asked us to explain quantum physics in Sanskrit.</p><p>“Uh… we haven’t really thought about it?”</p><p>She didn’t lecture us. She didn’t tell us we were wasting our time. She just said something like: “You’re in third year. You have the skills. And this will help in the future. Why not try?”</p><p>That conversation….probably 15 minutes max….completely changed our trajectory. Within a few weeks, we’d gone from “let’s play one more round” to “let’s learn transfer learning.”</p><p>Looking back, I don’t know if Dr. Nimbhorkar knew she was lighting a fire under two kids who were primarily focused on perfecting their PUBG strategy. But she did. And because of that push, we ended up publishing two papers, learning deep learning from scratch, and actually doing something with our third year beyond video games.</p><p>So thank you, Dr. Sejal Santosh Nimbhorkar, for the motivation, the push, and for opening our eyes at exactly the right time. This one’s for you.</p><h3>The Pre-ChatGPT Stone Age (Also Known As 2021)</h3><p>Now let me set the actual scene: It’s mid 2021. There’s no ChatGPT. No Claude. No Gemini. When your neural network threw an error, you had exactly two options:</p><ol><li>Stack Overflow</li><li>Suffer</li></ol><p>We chose both, extensively.</p><p>COVID-19 was still raging and we were just computer science students who had just been inspired to do research and thought: “Hey, we learned about CNNs in class. How hard could medical imaging be?”</p><h3>Two projects and zero biology</h3><p>We somehow convinced ourselves to take on two research projects:</p><p><a href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/364821917_Detection_of_Pneumonia_and_COVID-19_from_Chest_X-Ray_Images_Using_Neural_Networks_and_Deep_Learning"><strong>Project 1</strong>:</a> Detect COVID-19 and Pneumonia from chest X-rays using AlexNet, InceptionV3, ResNet50, and VGG19</p><p><a href="https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/9847945"><strong>Project 2</strong>:</a> Classify lung cancer histopathology images (benign vs. adenocarcinoma vs. squamous cell carcinoma) using six different architectures</p><p>Our qualifications for these projects? We could code in Python and had watched exactly one YouTube video about transfer learning.</p><p>The dataset descriptions might as well have been in ancient Greek:</p><ul><li>“5,863 X-ray images of pneumonia cases”</li><li>“25,000 histopathological images with lung tissue samples”</li><li>“Non-small cell lung cancer classification”</li></ul><p>What’s a histopathological image? What’s the difference between bacterial and viral pneumonia? What the hell is “non-small cell” lung cancer as opposed to… small cell? Big cell? Medium cell?</p><p>We had no idea.</p><h3>The Stack Overflow Learning Curve</h3><p>You become very familiar with the top contributors on Stack Overflow. Every single implementation decision required archaeological research through:</p><ul><li>15 year old TensorFlow forum posts</li><li>Research papers with code that didn’t run anymore</li><li>GitHub repos with README files that said “works on my machine”</li><li>Random blog posts from 2018 that were 80% helpful and 20% deprecated</li></ul><p>I remember spending a few hours trying to understand why our InceptionV3 model expected 299x299 images when our dataset had 768x768 images. The solution? Literally just resize them. But finding that answer required reading through three papers and several Stack Overflow threads.</p><p>The workflow was:</p><ol><li>Try to implement something from a paper</li><li>Get an error you don’t understand</li><li>Copy error into Google</li><li>Read 12 Stack Overflow answers</li><li>Try random solutions until something works</li><li>Move to next error</li><li>Repeat 45 times</li></ol><h3>When Your Model Works But You Don’t Know Why</h3><p>The beautiful/terrifying thing about transfer learning is that it works. Like, really well. Even when you have no idea what you’re doing.</p><p>We’d train a VGG19 model on chest X-rays, and it would achieve 96% accuracy. We’d look at the confusion matrix showing near-perfect classification of COVID vs. pneumonia vs. normal, and we’d celebrate.</p><p>But if you asked us “What features is the model actually detecting?” Silence.</p><p>“What makes this X-ray show pneumonia?” More silence.</p><p>“Could you explain the biological mechanism?” LOL</p><h3>Become a member</h3><p>The breakthrough moments weren’t about biology….they were about computational understanding. Like when we figured out why Adam optimizer crushed SGD for VGG19 (34.5% accuracy increase on one dataset). Or when we realized ResNet50’s skip connections were actually hurting performance for our specific use case.</p><p>We discovered that EfficientNetB2 could achieve 99% accuracy with far fewer parameters than the traditional models. We learned that adding two dense layers with 1024 and 512 neurons to ResNet50 could bump its accuracy by 5%.</p><p>These were our insights, earned through running the same model architecture seventeen different ways and comparing the results in Excel spreadsheets that would make any data scientist today cry.</p><h3>The Biology We Didn’t Learn (But Probably Should Have)</h3><p>Here’s a confession: We published two papers on medical imaging and I still couldn’t tell you what lung adenocarcinoma actually was without Googling it.</p><p>We knew the computational patterns:</p><ul><li>Benign tissue had certain pixel distributions</li><li>Adenocarcinoma showed different morphological features in the embeddings</li><li>Squamous cell carcinoma clustered differently in the feature space</li></ul><p>But what those actually meant biologically? No clue.</p><p>We were like linguists who could translate a language perfectly without knowing what any of the words meant. The model could identify cancer with 99% accuracy. We could optimize the architecture, tune the hyperparameters, reduce the trainable parameters from 23 million to 6,000.</p><p>But if you showed us an actual tissue slide and asked “is this cancer?” we’d probably guess wrong.</p><h3>The Paper Writing Process (AKA Citation Hell)</h3><p>Writing academic papers without AI assistance is its own special kind of assignment. Every sentence required:</p><ul><li>Reading 5–10 other papers to see how they phrased similar ideas</li><li>Manually formatting citations in the exact format the journal wanted</li><li>Drawing architecture diagrams in PowerPoint (yes, PowerPoint)</li><li>Creating tables comparing our results to previous work while being careful not to cherry pick</li></ul><p>The literature review sections took a few weeks. We’d read papers like “Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition” just to understand how He et al. described their architecture, so we could describe ours similarly without plagiarizing.</p><h3>The Validation That Made It Worth It</h3><p>Six months after we started, we had:</p><ul><li>Two published papers at conferences</li><li>A COVID-19 detection model with 94.25% accuracy</li><li>A lung cancer classification system hitting 99% on EfficientNetB2</li><li>A deep understanding of transfer learning, CNNs, and optimization</li><li>Absolutely zero understanding of actual lung pathology</li></ul><p>Both papers got accepted. Other researchers started citing our work.</p><p>People were building on what we’d learned through trial, error, and countless Stack Overflow searches. Our lack of biological knowledge didn’t matter….the computational contributions were valid.</p><h3>Looking Back From The ChatGPT Era</h3><p>Now it’s 2026. I’m doing computational biology research at NYU using ChatGPT regularly. The difference is absurd.</p><p>A problem that would have taken me four hours of Stack Overflow archaeology in 2021? Now takes 4 minutes.</p><p>Sometimes I wonder if doing research in 2021….was better training. We had to understand everything deeply because there was no AI to explain it. We had to read the source code because there was no chatbot to debug it. We had to think through the architecture choices because there was no assistant to suggest alternatives.</p><h3>For Students Reading This (In The AI Era)</h3><p>If you’re learning ML/DL now, you have tools we could only dream about in 2021. Use them. Embrace them. Don’t feel guilty about it.</p><p>But here’s my advice:</p><p>Try breaking things the old way at least once.</p><p>Not because suffering builds character (though it kind of does), but because the understanding you gain from fighting through problems manually is different from the understanding you gain from asking an AI.</p><p>We learned deep learning the hard way ….through error messages, failed experiments, and architectures that didn’t work for reasons we had to figure out ourselves. It was frustrating, time-consuming, and occasionally made us question our life choices.</p><p>Your path will be different. Probably better in most ways. But don’t skip the struggle entirely.</p><h3>The Real Victory</h3><p>The COVID-19 detection model we built in 2021 probably helped exactly zero patients. The lung cancer classifier never made it to a hospital. Our biological understanding remained embarrassingly shallow.</p><p>But we learned how to learn. We learned how to read research papers critically, implement complex architectures, optimize for both accuracy and efficiency, and push through when everything breaks.</p><p>Would I do it again? Absolutely. But I’m grateful I don’t have to.</p><p><strong>PS</strong>: If you’ve made it this far and you’re thinking “wow, they really published papers without understanding the biology?” Yes. Yes we did. Academic peer review is more focused on computational methodology than biological insight, apparently. Don’t @ me, I’m just being honest.</p><p><strong>PPS:</strong> Shoutout to <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/jnimbhor/">Jeet Nimbhorkar</a> and <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/k-jeevesh-71003416b/">Jeevesh Konindala</a> for being the best teammates anyone could ask for during this journey. From PUBG squads to research paper co-authors….we made it work!! Couldn’t have done it without you guys.</p><p><strong>PPPS</strong>: If you want to see the actual code, both papers are published and available. Fair warning: the code quality reflects what happens when you learn everything from Stack Overflow and trial and error. It works. Is it elegant? Absolutely not.</p>
