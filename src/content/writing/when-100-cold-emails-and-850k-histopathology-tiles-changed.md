<p><em>A student’s honest account of breaking into cancer research at NYU and why personal loss fuels the best science.</em></p><p>I still remember staring at my inbox after sending my 47th cold email, watching days tick by with nothing but silence. No responses. No acknowledgments. Just the rumble of the D train passing by my Brooklyn apartment. (Sorry not sorry, I’m always gonna include NYC references in my work)</p><p>It was April 2024, and I had just sent my 100th cold email to professors across NYU, desperately trying to break into AI research. My grandfather had passed away from cancer the year before, my grandmother is battling both Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s, and I was determined to contribute something meaningful to the fight against these diseases that had torn through my family.</p><p>99 professors never replied. One did.</p><p><strong>Professor Aristotelis Tsirigos. </strong>(The Greeks really said “let’s invent philosophy, mathematics, AND save this kid’s research dreams” and honestly, respect!)</p><h3>The Email That Changed Everything</h3><figure><img loading="lazy" alt="" src="/writing/when-100-cold-emails-and-850k-histopathology-tiles-changed/bf9ce23908ac.png" /><figcaption>The first response!!</figcaption></figure><p>He spelled my name “Aravid” instead of “Aravind,” but honestly, after 99 rejections, he could have called me “Random Brooklyn Kid #69” and I still would have shown up to his office faster than the MTA could take me there.</p><p>I received the next steps email a week later, attached was a 42-page paper titled <em>“Mapping the landscape of histomorphological cancer phenotypes using self-supervised learning on unannotated pathology slides.”</em> The methodology involved something called Histomorphological Phenotype Learning (HPL), Barlow Twins self-supervised learning, and analyzing 850,000+ histopathology image tiles from lung cancer patients.</p><p>I had never used a High-Performance Computing (HPC) cluster. I had never implemented self-supervised learning from scratch.</p><p>“Yes, absolutely. This should be do-able”.</p><h3>What They Don’t Tell You About Computational Pathology</h3><p>The paper made it sound straightforward: extract tiles from whole slide images, train a self-supervised model, cluster the embeddings, and classify cancer subtypes. Easy, right?</p><p>What they don’t mention in the methods section:</p><ul><li><strong>The data pipeline from hell:</strong> 850K+ histopathology tiles stored in HDF5 format, each requiring careful preprocessing to remove background tissue and normalize staining variations</li><li><strong>HPC cluster learning curve:</strong> NYU’s Greene cluster documentation that assumes you already know SLURM, module loading, and GPU allocation strategies</li><li><strong>Self-supervised learning mysteries:</strong> Understanding why Barlow Twins works, how to reshape 128-dimensional embeddings into CNN inputs, and debugging CUDA memory errors.</li></ul><p>I spent the first three weeks just understanding the data format, the pipeline and figuring out how to submit jobs to the HPC cluster. Nicolas, a Scientist in the lab, became my lifeline, patiently explaining why my jobs kept failing and helping me navigate the computational infrastructure.</p><figure><img loading="lazy" alt="" src="/writing/when-100-cold-emails-and-850k-histopathology-tiles-changed/28c0b41ec3f6.png" /><figcaption>The pipeline used for the HPL study</figcaption></figure><h3>The Midnight Debugging Sessions</h3><p>Month two was when things got real. I was attempting to recreate the original Barlow Twins self-supervised learning pipeline that generated those 128-dimensional embeddings. The model architecture itself looked brutal on first glance.</p><p>I remember one particularly painful night, debugging why my InceptionV3 model was achieving only 65% AUC when the original paper reported 93% on the same TCGA dataset. The issue? I was treating the 128-dimensional embeddings as if they were RGB images. The model was looking for spatial patterns in embeddings that had already been compressed to semantic representations.</p><p>That realization was my first research insight.</p><h3>The Personal Stakes</h3><p>The failed experiments and crashed jobs would have been easier to give up on if this was just another class project. But every time I stared at those pink and purple tissue sections on my screen, I remembered why I started this work.</p><p>Self-supervised learning suddenly wasn’t just an interesting AI technique. It was a way to find patterns in cancer tissue that human pathologists might miss, potentially leading to better diagnoses and treatments. The Histomorphological Phenotype Learning approach could identify tissue patterns associated with better survival outcomes, like high lymphocyte infiltration and inflammatory cell signatures.</p><h3>The Breakthrough</h3><p>After three months of recreating the original methodology, I finally understood why it worked so well. The beauty of the HPL approach wasn’t in complex deep learning architectures, it was in the interpretability. Each histomorphological phenotype cluster corresponded to actual cancer biology: acinar patterns, papillary growth, solid tumor regions, and immune cell infiltration.</p><p>My extension using InceptionV3 directly on the embeddings achieved 78% accuracy, which was respectable but missed the elegance of the original clustering approach. More importantly, I had learned about:</p><ul><li><strong>Self-supervised learning:</strong> How models can learn meaningful representations without labeled data</li><li><strong>Computational pathology:</strong> The intersection of AI and medical imaging</li><li><strong>Research methodology:</strong> How to read papers critically, reproduce results, and extend existing work</li><li><strong>HPC systems:</strong> Managing computational resources for large-scale experiments</li></ul><h3>The Payoff</h3><p>Six months after that first email from Professor Tsirigos, something unexpected happened. My research experience at NYU Langone, combined with the GitHub repository documenting my work, started opening doors.</p><p>Interview calls came from:</p><ul><li><strong>Ataraxis AI: </strong>A biotech startup working on AI-powered drug discovery</li><li><strong>Pathos: </strong>Digital pathology platform using computer vision</li><li><strong>Gen Bio: </strong>Computational biology company</li><li><strong>CZ Biohub: </strong>Chan Zuckerberg Initiative’s research institute</li></ul><h3>Looking Back</h3><p>That experience taught me more about research than any coursework ever could. Not just the technical aspects, though learning to wrangle 850K histopathology tiles and implement self-supervised learning from scratch was invaluable, but the human elements.</p><p>Research is personal. The best science comes from genuine curiosity and real stakes. When you’re motivated… you want to contribute to something bigger than yourself, push through the debugging sessions, the failed experiments and the imposter syndrome.</p><p>Professor Tsirigos could have ignored my email like 99 others did. Instead, he gave an unknown student a chance to prove themselves. That single response changed my trajectory completely.</p><h3>For Students Reading This</h3><p>If you’re trying to break into research, especially in healthcare AI, here’s what I learned:</p><ol><li><strong>Cold emails work, but volume matters:</strong> 100+ emails got me 1 response. That 1% response rate changed everything.</li><li><strong>Reproduce before you innovate:</strong> Understanding existing work deeply is more valuable than jumping straight to “novel” ideas.</li><li><strong>Personal motivation sustains you:</strong> Technical challenges can be overcome when you really care about the problem.</li><li><strong>Unpaid work can pay dividends:</strong> The experience and portfolio you build matter more than immediate compensation.</li><li><strong>Find your Nicolas:</strong> Every lab has someone willing to help newcomers navigate the computational infrastructure.</li></ol><p>The GitHub repository with all my code, documentation, and results is <a href="https://github.com/AravindKurapati/TCGA-Lung-Inception-NYU-HPC">here</a>. My work definitely did not revolutionize cancer diagnosis, but it opened doors I never expected and taught me that sometimes the best research comes from the most personal places.</p><p>Cancer took my grandfather, but maybe the patterns reseachers find in histopathology images will help someone else’s family have a different outcome. That possibility makes every learning and debugging session worth it.</p><p><em>Special thanks to Professor Aristotelis Tsirigos for being the 1 in 100 who responded, and to Nicolas for the patient guidance through computational pathology and HPC systems. This work was supported by the NYU Langone Health Department of Pathology and NYU High Performance Computing resources.</em></p>
